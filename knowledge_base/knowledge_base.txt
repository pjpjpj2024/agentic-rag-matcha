SECTION 1
History

Powdered green tea reached Japan by way of China in the twelfth century, brought back by a Zen monk who had studied abroad and returned with tea seeds. He established a devotional approach to tea preparation that treated the process itself as a spiritual practice. Roughly a century later, during the Kamakura period, Zen monks began drinking this powdered tea specifically to stay alert during long stretches of meditation, and it gradually became woven into monastic ritual. By the sixteenth century, a tea master serving under a powerful military ruler formalized the practice into a distinct aesthetic style built around restraint, simplicity, and quiet, a style that remains influential in Japan today.

SECTION 2
Cultivation

The tea plants used for this style of tea are grown under shade before harvest, a technique that boosts chlorophyll levels and gives the leaves their vivid green color. Only the youngest growth is picked: the buds and the top few layers of leaves on each plant. This selective, labor-intensive harvesting is part of what sets the crop apart from ordinary tea production.

SECTION 3
Production

After picking, the leaves are steamed almost immediately to stop oxidation from setting in. The stems and veins are then removed, and what remains is ground very slowly using traditional stone mills. Because each of these steps is done by hand or with slow, deliberate methods rather than mass processing, the finished product is significantly more expensive than typical loose-leaf green tea, making it one of the more expensive teas available on the market.

SECTION 4
Grades

Quality is judged mainly by color and flavor, and the finest grade is reserved for ceremonial use, meant to be drunk plain without sweeteners or additions. A second, slightly more bitter grade is intended for cooking and mixed drinks, showing up in ice cream, lattes, and even cocktails. The distinction between these grades matters both for taste and for how the tea is meant to be consumed.

SECTION 5
Health Benefits

This tea carries a higher caffeine content than most other green teas. In moderate amounts, it's associated with improved focus and memory, and its antioxidant content is high enough that some research points to possible cancer-fighting effects. It's also linked to reduced stress and anxiety, and the catechin compounds it contains may help lower blood pressure and cholesterol.

SECTION 6
Tea Ceremony

The ceremony takes place in a dedicated tea room, often a small standalone structure built with plain, natural materials to give an impression of rustic refinement. The room typically includes an alcove for a hanging scroll or flower arrangement, and a small sunken hearth used to heat water in colder months. Guests enter through a deliberately low doorway meant to encourage humility. The host brings in the utensils, offers sweets, and prepares tea by whisking powdered leaves into hot water; on certain occasions a thicker, more concentrated version is served instead of the usual thin, frothy style. Once the tea is finished, guests are invited to examine and discuss the utensils before the gathering ends. The ceremony is guided by four core principles: harmony between guests and objects, mutual respect, cleanliness (rooted in ritual hand-washing and mouth-rinsing before entering), and a sense of tranquility built up through the long, careful use of the same implements over time.

SECTION 7
Zen Buddhism

The custom of ritual tea drinking originated in China and was first practiced in Japan by Zen monks, who used tea to stay awake through extended meditation sessions. Over time it became tied to Zen ritual more broadly, including ceremonies honoring the tradition's first patriarch. By the fifteenth century, this practice had expanded beyond strict religious use into gatherings where friends discussed art, calligraphy, and the tea utensils themselves, though its roots in Zen discipline and mindfulness remained central to its character.

SECTION 8
Preparation

Rather than being steeped and strained like typical loose-leaf tea, this tea is a fine powder whisked directly into hot water using a bamboo brush until frothy, and the suspended tea itself is drunk rather than removed. The resulting brew has a distinct flavor profile: slightly nutty and grassy, with a mild bitterness that varies depending on grade. This method of preparation, powder suspended rather than steeped, is what gives the tea its characteristic texture and taste.
